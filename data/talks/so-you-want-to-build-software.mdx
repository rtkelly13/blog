# A SHORT HISTORY OF COMPUTING

- **1940s–60s** — computers filled entire *rooms*, cost millions, run by teams in lab coats.
- **1970s–80s** — the *personal* computer arrives on desks at home.
- **1990s** — the **internet** connects them all together.
- **2000s** — the **smartphone** puts one in everyone's pocket.
- **Today** — **AI** that writes, draws, and talks.

> The phone in your pocket is **millions of times** more powerful than the computer that guided Apollo to the Moon.

???

[⏱ 14–19 · HISTORY] Keep this FAST and visual — 4–5 milestones, don't lecture.
- The point isn't the dates; it's the *pace of change*: in one lifetime, room →
  pocket. "By the time you're my age, today's tech will look ancient."
- The Apollo comparison always lands — let them sit with it for a beat.
- Pivot: "So who builds all this? That's my job."

# WHAT IS SOFTWARE ENGINEERING?

Every app, website, and game you use was **built by people**.

Software engineering is:

- Turning an idea into instructions a computer can follow
- Solving real problems for real people
- Mostly **thinking and figuring things out** — the typing is the easy bit

It's less like a film hacker, more like **building with invisible LEGO**.

???

[⏱ 19–24 · WHAT IS SE + PARENTS] Interactive beat (device-free):
- **"Shout out what your parents/carers do for work."** Take 4–5.
- For each: "Do they use a computer or an app to do it?" — nurse (records),
  shop (tills), driver (sat-nav/routing), builder (orders).
- Land it: **software is everywhere, and someone built every bit of it.** That
  someone has a job like mine.
- Set up the key idea for the next activity: our job is turning an idea into
  **exact instructions**.

# 🍞 TRY IT: MAKING TOAST

**Your job:** in your groups, write the **exact steps** to make a piece of toast.

**My job:** I'm the computer. I'll do *exactly* what you say — nothing more, nothing less.

> Rules: no step-skipping, no "obviously". If you don't say it, I can't do it.

<OrderedActions />

???

[⏱ 28–46 · TOAST ACTIVITY 🍞 · ~18 min · device-free]
- If the room has devices: open the "make toast" activity from /admin (Audience
  participation → Put-it-in-order). Groups submit their steps; the wall fills
  live and the answer reveals on the timer. Otherwise run it on paper as below.
Based on discovere.org/engineering-activities/making-toast.
1. (2m) Split into groups of 3–4. Each group writes numbered steps to make toast.
2. (8m) Groups write. Circulate, encourage precision.
3. (6m) Pick 1–2 groups. Read their steps aloud and **follow them literally** —
   comedically. "Put bread in toaster" but they never said open the bag → jam
   the whole bag in. "Press down" → they never said plug it in.
4. (2m) DEBRIEF — the whole point:
   - Computers do **exactly** what you say — no common sense.
   - Breaking a problem into precise, ordered steps = **decomposition**, and it's
     the single biggest part of my actual job.
   - "You just did software engineering. No computer required."
Props: a loaf + toaster if allowed, or mime it. Keep it light and funny.

# HOW SOFTWARE GETS MADE

Building software is a **loop**, not a straight line. We ship a small piece, learn from it, and improve.

<Diagram type="mermaid" caption="The build–ship–learn loop">
{`flowchart LR
  A[Idea] --> B[Plan]
  B --> C[Write Code]
  C --> D[Test It]
  D --> E[Ship It]
  E --> F[Get Feedback]
  F --> B`}
</Diagram>

We go around this loop again and again — sometimes many times a day.

???

[⏱ 52–58 · HOW IT'S MADE] Connect to toast: "same idea — small precise steps,
then check it worked."
- The loop is the rhythm of the job. We don't build the whole thing at once; we
  ship small and learn.

# A DAY IN THE LIFE

It's **not** 8 hours of silent typing in a dark room. Most of my day is a cycle:

- 🔎 **Discover** a problem — something's broken or missing
- 💡 **Propose** a solution — and talk it through with the team
- ⌨️ **Write** the change — the actual code
- ✅ **Test** it — prove it works before real people use it

**Tools I use every day:** an editor (VS Code), Git (a time machine for code), and — increasingly — **AI assistants**.

???

[⏱ 58–66 · DAY IN THE LIFE] This is the "what do I actually do all day" section
they asked about.
- Walk discover → propose → write → test with a concrete example from your week.
- Who I work for + **other employers**: games studios, banks, the NHS, startups,
  the company down the road — every industry needs engineers.
- Name-drop the tools briefly; mention AI honestly (a helper, not a replacement —
  you still have to know what you're asking for).
- We read & talk **more** than we type.

# THE ROLE OF AI

AI is the biggest change in my job right now — but it's a **power tool, not a replacement**.

- ✍️ It writes first drafts of code — I still decide *what* to build and *check* it's right
- ⚡ It speeds up the boring bits, so I spend more time on the hard thinking
- ⚠️ It's **confidently wrong** sometimes — you need the fundamentals to catch it

**The skill that matters more than ever:** knowing what to ask for, and telling whether the answer is any good.

???

[⏱ 66–69 · THE ROLE OF AI] They'll be curious — be honest and balanced.
- Frame it as a tool that makes good engineers faster, not one that removes the
  need for them.
- The "confidently wrong" point is key: you can't check AI's work without
  understanding the basics — so learning fundamentals matters MORE, not less.
- Reassure: it's an opportunity if you learn to work with it. Tie to the toast
  activity — you still have to know the exact steps to spot a wrong one.

# IT'S A TEAM SPORT

The biggest myth: that engineers work alone. Software is built by a **team of roles**:

- 👩‍💻 **Engineers** — build it
- 🎨 **Designers** — decide how it looks and feels
- 🧪 **Testers / QA** — try to break it before users do
- 📋 **Product managers** — decide what to build and why
- 📊 **Data** & 🔒 **security** people, and more

**Communicating clearly is a superpower.** Code talks to computers; words build things with people.

???

[⏱ 82–87 · TEAM SPORT + OTHER JOBS] Kill the lone-genius myth.
- Walk the roles — "if you love art, design; if you love finding faults, QA; if
  you love organising, product." There's a way in for lots of different people.
- The point: you don't have to be *the coder* to work in tech.
